Problem (short)

Hidden state \(s_t=(x_t,v_t)\), action \(a_t\in\{-1,+1\}\), dynamics

\[x_{t+1}=x_t+v_t,\qquad v_{t+1}=v_t+a_t.\]

Policy sees only \(o_t=x_t\). Minimize \(x_{t+2}^2+0.1v_{t+2}^2\).

  1. Two histories / states: same \(o_t\), different optimal actions.
  2. Prove no memoryless \(\pi(a_t\mid o_t)\) is always optimal.
  3. Smallest sufficient policy input.
  4. How treating \(o\) as \(s\) changes \(p(\tau)\).

\[ x_{t+2} = x_t + 2v_t + a_t \]

You

Why did we think that \(a_t\) and \(a_{t+1}\) both will be optimal?

Tutor good question

Short answer: “Optimal action at time \(t\)” means: the action that leads to the best possible cost if the rest of the plan is also played as well as possible. That is the definition of an optimal action in sequential decision-making — not “\(a_t\) alone, with a random or fixed future.”

Why both steps matter here

The thing we minimize is only written at time \(t+2\):

\[ J = x_{t+2}^2 + 0.1\, v_{t+2}^2 \]

Getting to \((x_{t+2}, v_{t+2})\) takes two decisions: \(a_t\) then \(a_{t+1}\). So the true best first move is

\[ a_t^\star = \arg\min_{a_t\in\{-1,+1\}} \Big( \min_{a_{t+1}\in\{-1,+1\}} \big[ x_{t+2}(a_t)^2 + 0.1\, v_{t+2}(a_t,a_{t+1})^2 \big] \Big). \]

Inner min = “best second move given the first.” Outer min = “best first move assuming that.”

What would go wrong if we didn’t?

  • If we fixed \(a_{t+1}\) somehow (say always \(+1\)), we might call a bad \(a_t\) “optimal” just because we crippled the future on purpose.
  • Part (a) asks for optimal actions under the true objective — i.e. an optimal controller that sees the full hidden state \((x,v)\).
  • Later, part (b) says: even that optimal full-state controller wants different \(a_t\) in two situations that look identical to a memoryless \(\pi(a\mid o)\). That’s the punchline.

Tiny analogy

Chess: “Is this the best first move?” means “best if I also play the rest optimally,” not “best assuming I blunder on move 2.”

What we are not assuming

  • We are not assuming the observation-only policy knows \(v\) or plans perfectly. That policy is what we’re attacking.
  • We temporarily pretend an oracle sees \((x,v)\) and picks the best two-step plan — only to show those oracles disagree across aliased states.

Part (b) — what you are really proving

Tutor · explain part (b) (clear, not spoon-fed)

Setup you already have

From (a): there exist two hidden states that look identical to the agent (\(o_t=0\)) but disagree on which action is best. Call that disagreement “the policy’s dilemma.”

What a memoryless policy even is

A policy \(\pi(a_t\mid o_t)\) is allowed to look at only the current observation. It has no \(v\), no past \(o\)’s, no past \(a\)’s. So whenever \(o_t\) takes a particular value, \(\pi\) is locked into one behavioral rule for that value — the same rule every time that observation appears.

What “optimal for every reachable hidden state” would require

If such a \(\pi\) were truly optimal everywhere it can end up, then in each reachable hidden state it would have to choose (or put probability 1 on) an action that is optimal for that hidden state.

Where the tension is

Those two requirements pull against each other:

  • Memoryless ⇒ “same \(o\) ⇒ same rule.”
  • Always-optimal ⇒ “different hidden needs ⇒ different rule.”

Part (b) is just making that tension formal: show they cannot both be true, using your concrete pair from (a) as the witness.

How proofs of this flavor usually feel

You don’t invent new dynamics. You assume the strong claim (“there exists a memoryless \(\pi\) that is always optimal”), then ask what that \(\pi\) must do at the shared observation — and show that whatever it does, it betrays at least one of the two hidden states. That betrayal is the contradiction.

Things you do not need

  • A formula for \(\pi\) for every \(o\).
  • Solving the whole POMDP.
  • Long algebra beyond (a).

You need a clean logical sentence that connects: shared observation → forced shared behavior → cannot match both optimal actions.

One concept from lecture to keep nearby

Markov / sufficiency: if the input to \(\pi\) leaves out something the optimal action depends on (here, velocity), then no function of that input alone can track the optimal action in every situation. Part (b) is that sentence, specialized to this robot.

You

Give the answer for part (b).

Tutor part (b) answer

Claim. No memoryless policy \(\pi(a_t\mid o_t)\) is optimal for every reachable hidden state.

Proof (by contradiction).

From (a), the reachable hidden states \(s^+=(0,+1)\) and \(s^-=(0,-1)\) share the same observation \(o_t=0\), but have different optimal actions:

\[ a^\star(s^+)=-1, \qquad a^\star(s^-)=+1. \]

Assume, for contradiction, that some memoryless \(\pi(a\mid o)\) is optimal at every reachable hidden state. In particular it is optimal at both \(s^+\) and \(s^-\).

Because \(\pi\) is memoryless, its action distribution at these two states is the same object: \(\pi(\cdot\mid o=0)\).

Deterministic case. If \(\pi\) picks a single action \(a_0\) at \(o=0\), then \(a_0\) cannot equal both \(-1\) and \(+1\). So \(a_0\) is suboptimal for at least one of \(s^+,s^-\), contradicting optimality everywhere.

Stochastic case. If \(\pi(\cdot\mid 0)\) puts positive probability on both actions, then at \(s^+\) it takes the suboptimal action \(+1\) with positive probability (and symmetrically at \(s^-\) it takes \(-1\) with positive probability). An optimal policy for a given hidden state must put probability 1 on an optimal action for that state. Contradiction again.

Therefore no such memoryless \(\pi\) exists.

One-line moral: same observation forces the same \(\pi\)-behavior; different hidden states need different optimal actions; those cannot both hold.

What’s right about your doubt

If the policy must choose \(a_t\) now, it cannot take \(a_t\) or \(o_{t+1}\) as inputs. Those aren’t available yet when it decides. So as an input for time-\(t\) action selection, \((o_t,a_t,o_{t+1})\) is the wrong time stamp — your discomfort is justified.

Concept reminder — sufficiency is about “what do I know when I act?”

At the moment you pick \(a_t\), you already have:

  • current observation \(o_t = x_t\)
  • whatever is in the past: previous observations and previous actions

So a finite-history candidate should look like something ending at \(t\), e.g. involving \(o_{t-1}\), \(a_{t-1}\), \(o_t\) — not the future.

Second concept — what are we trying to recover?

Optimal \(a_t\) depends on the hidden state \((x_t, v_t)\). We already see \(x_t\). So the only missing piece is \(v_t\). Ask: which past bits let you reconstruct \(v_t\) from the dynamics?

\[ x_t = x_{t-1} + v_{t-1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_{t-1} = o_t - o_{t-1} \]
\[ v_t = v_{t-1} + a_{t-1} \]

Read those two lines and decide: do you need only two positions? Or also the last action?

About your triple, shifted

The same three symbols with the index moved to the past — information like “last obs, last action, current obs” — is a different candidate than “current obs, current action, next obs.” Don’t throw away the idea; fix the time index.

You

Tutor part (c) done

Yes. At decision time \(t\), recover the hidden state as:

\[ x_t = o_t, \qquad v_{t-1} = o_t - o_{t-1}, \qquad v_t = v_{t-1} + a_{t-1} = (o_t - o_{t-1}) + a_{t-1}. \]

So \((x_t,v_t)\) is known ⇒ any optimal action that depends on the full state is representable as a function of \((o_{t-1},a_{t-1},o_t)\).

Why this is (essentially) minimal as a history

  • Drop \(o_t\): you don’t even know current position.
  • Drop \(o_{t-1}\): you can’t form \(v_{t-1}=o_t-o_{t-1}\).
  • Drop \(a_{t-1}\): two positions give \(v_{t-1}\), not \(v_t\) (velocity already changed by the last action).

Equally valid “augmented observation” answer: let the sensor give \(\tilde{o}_t=(x_t,v_t)\) directly. That’s the full Markov state — also sufficient, and smaller as a vector (2 numbers). The history above is how you build an equivalent state when you only ever measure \(x\).

Part (d) — factorization of \(p(\tau)\)

Tutor · explain part (d)

Ask: explain why treating observations as states changes the factorization of the trajectory distribution.

Recall the true (hidden-state) factorization from lecture:

\[ p(\tau) = p(s_1)\prod_t \pi(a_t\mid s_t)\, p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \]

with Markov dynamics in \(s\).

What people sometimes write if they pretend \(o\) is \(s\):

\[ p(\tau) \stackrel{?}{=} p(o_1)\prod_t \pi(a_t\mid o_t)\, p(o_{t+1}\mid o_t,a_t) \]

Your job: say what goes wrong with that second line in this problem (or in POMDPs generally). You don’t need a long derivation — name which factor is unjustified and why, using what we learned about \(v\) / history.

You

Both. They need one previous obs more to determine both policy and transition.

Tutor part (d) done small tighten

Correct call: both factors fail.

1. Policy factor \(\pi(a_t\mid o_t)\)

From (a)–(b), optimal action is not a function of \(o_t\) alone. The true generative story for a competent agent is more like \(\pi(a_t\mid o_{t-1},a_{t-1},o_t)\) (or \(\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\) with \(s_t=(x_t,v_t)\)). Writing \(\prod \pi(a_t\mid o_t)\) pretends a memoryless observation-policy that we already proved cannot be optimal everywhere.

2. Transition factor \(p(o_{t+1}\mid o_t,a_t)\)

This is the Markov property claim for observations. It is false here. Next position is

\[ o_{t+1}=x_{t+1}=x_t+v_t=o_t+v_t, \]

so you need \(v_t\), which is not determined by \((o_t,a_t)\) alone (same \(o_t\), opposite \(v_t\) ⇒ different \(o_{t+1}\); and \(a_t\) doesn’t even enter \(x_{t+1}\)). After marginalizing the hidden state, generally

\[ p(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t}) \;\neq\; p(o_{t+1}\mid o_t,a_t). \]

Tighten your “one previous obs” line

From (c), one previous observation is not enough by itself. You need previous observation and previous action:

\[ v_t=(o_t-o_{t-1})+a_{t-1}. \]

So the honest observation-trajectory factorization uses history (or an explicit latent \(s\)), not the MDP-style product in \(o\) alone.

Clean (d) answer in one paragraph

If observations are treated as states, people copy the MDP factorization \(p(o_1)\prod_t\pi(a_t\mid o_t)p(o_{t+1}\mid o_t,a_t)\). Both products are unjustified: the optimal (and even Markov-sufficient) policy depends on more than \(o_t\), and the observation process is not Markov in \((o_t,a_t)\) because velocity is latent. The correct latent-state factorization keeps \(p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\) and either conditions \(\pi\) on \(s_t\) or on a sufficient history of \((o,a)\).
